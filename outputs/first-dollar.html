01
THE STARTING POINT

A good first investment
starts outside the market.

For your situation, the first decision is how much of your money is truly long-term. Your remaining nine months in the US and your investment horizon are two different clocks.

YOUR CONTEXT

California resident · Vietnamese citizen · STEM OPT · US income-tax resident · About nine more months in the US. The main guide covers US accounts and US-domiciled investments. A US-domiciled fund can hold companies worldwide.

If you need the money in nine months

Prioritize an FDIC-insured high-yield savings account or Treasury bills maturing before you need the cash. Moving costs, tuition, taxes, a deposit and your emergency reserve do not belong in stock funds. A stock ETF can be easy to sell while still being worth much less than you paid.

If you can leave it invested for 10+ years

A low-cost, diversified stock index fund, optionally paired with high-quality bonds, is a reasonable starting framework. You do not need to predict the next winning company. You do need to tolerate a large decline without being forced to sell.

A SENSIBLE DEFAULT TO INVESTIGATE

Fidelity + a cash account + a simple portfolio.

For a US-based beginner, my starting broker choice is Fidelity: low trading costs and recurring dollar-based ETF purchases. Choose the account’s tax treatment first. For long-term stock exposure, investigate VT for a single global fund, or VTI + VXUS for separate US and international holdings. Add bonds according to your risk capacity. This is a starting framework, not a personalized allocation.

Put your next dollar in the right place

  1. 01
    Cover the basics.

    Pay essential bills, keep health coverage, make required debt payments and hold a starter cash buffer. A surprise bill should not force a stock sale.

  2. 02
    Check your employer match.

    Capture an affordable 401(k) match when the benefit is likely to vest. Read the vesting schedule carefully with a nine-month stay; your own contributions are yours, but some employer contributions may be forfeited when you leave.

  3. 03
    Eliminate expensive debt.

    Paying down a 20% credit-card balance avoids a known interest cost. Stock returns are uncertain. The debt-versus-match order depends on liquidity, the interest rate and vesting.

  4. 04
    Build your safety reserve.

    A common baseline is 3–6 months of essential expenses. Consider more when employment and visa status are linked, plus a separate relocation budget. Keep enough in a bank account for immediate access.

  5. 05
    Invest money with time to recover.

    Use eligible retirement accounts for retirement goals; use a taxable brokerage account for flexible long-term goals. Start with an amount you can repeat without drawing down your reserve.

Your nine-month test

Imagine stocks fall 40% the month before you need the money. If that would disrupt your plans, that money belongs in the short-term bucket. A long expected return does not cancel a near-term obligation.

A quick word on your STEM OPT and tax status

This guide takes your US income-tax residency as given; it does not determine it from your visa. F-1 status and tax residency are different systems. Student exclusions from the substantial-presence test usually cover the first five calendar years, with exceptions. Recheck residency for the actual tax year if your circumstances change. 02
HOW INVESTING WORKS

The container is not
the investment.

A broker, an account, a fund and a stock answer different questions. Keeping them separate makes almost everything else easier.

A

Stocks = ownership

You own part of a business. Returns come from changes in its value and distributions to owners. Profits can grow, but valuations can fall and companies can fail. A stock does not promise repayment.

B

Bonds = lending

You lend to a government or business. A bond specifies payments, subject to the issuer’s ability to pay. Before maturity, its price changes with interest rates, credit risk and other conditions.

C

Funds = a basket

Many investors pool money into a portfolio. A fund can own stocks, bonds, both, or something more complex. Owning several funds does not necessarily mean owning different assets.

The few ideas that do most of the work

Diversification reduces dependence

Thousands of companies reduce the damage from any one failure. They do not prevent a market crash. Geography and asset type matter too: holding ten technology funds can still be one concentrated bet.

Price is different from value

A $20 ETF is not “cheaper” than a $200 ETF in an economic sense. Share count is arbitrary; expenses, holdings and valuations matter. A 2-for-1 split changes the share count, not your wealth.

Total return is the useful measure

Total return includes price changes and distributions. A dividend is not free extra money: a distribution reduces the fund’s assets and, all else equal, its value. A high payout can coexist with a falling account balance.

Liquidity is different from safety

A stock ETF is liquid because you can usually sell it quickly. That says nothing about whether you will recover your purchase price. A Treasury held to maturity has a specified payment date; a bond fund keeps replacing holdings.

Compounding needs time

Reinvested gains can earn further gains. But losses compound too: a 50% decline requires a 100% gain to recover. Smooth illustrations are arithmetic, not how markets actually move.

Inflation changes the goal

If your account grows 5% and prices rise 3%, your purchasing power grows about 1.94%, before tax: 1.05 ÷ 1.03 − 1. A stable dollar balance can still buy less over time.

Roth versus traditional: compare tax rates, not buzzwords

A traditional contribution can be attractive if your marginal tax rate now exceeds the rate when you withdraw. Roth can be attractive in the reverse situation. Future rules, withdrawal timing and location matter. Do not assume a student with a salary is automatically in a low bracket, especially in California.

For 2026, direct Roth IRA contributions phase out at modified adjusted gross income of $153,000–$168,000 for single/head-of-household filers and $242,000–$252,000 for married filing jointly. Married-filing-separately rules are restrictive. For a single filer covered by a workplace plan, traditional IRA deductibility phases out at $81,000–$91,000. These are income tests, not account-balance limits. IRS · 2026 retirement limits ↗

Can I withdraw Roth IRA contributions if I need them?

Under federal ordering rules, regular Roth IRA contributions come out before conversions and earnings and generally can be withdrawn free of income tax and penalty. That does not mean the whole balance is freely accessible. Qualified earnings distributions generally require the five-tax-year rule plus age 59½ or another qualifying condition. Conversions have separate rules.

Using a Roth as an emergency account sacrifices scarce retirement space; withdrawing does not usually restore contribution room. Keep contribution records. Early taxable IRA distributions can also trigger a 10% federal additional tax unless an exception applies, and California can impose an additional tax. IRS · IRA distributions ↗ · California FTB · Retirement distribution taxes ↗

What about a backdoor Roth IRA, rollovers or stock from my employer?

A “backdoor Roth” combines a nondeductible traditional IRA contribution with a Roth conversion. Existing pretax traditional, SEP and SIMPLE IRA balances can make the conversion partly taxable under the pro-rata rule. It is not an extra contribution allowance. Use Form 8606 and get tax help before trying it.

When leaving an employer, compare keeping the old 401(k), a direct rollover to a new eligible plan, or a direct IRA rollover. Cashing it out can create tax and penalties. A pretax-to-Roth conversion is generally taxable.

RSUs, ESPPs and options are compensation arrangements, not a substitute for a diversified portfolio. Your salary and employer stock can fail together. Vesting, withholding and cost-basis adjustments need separate review; broker basis may omit income already taxed on a W-2.

Eight questions to settle before opening

  1. Eligibility: Does the broker accept your actual citizenship, current residence and visa documents? A US SSN does not guarantee approval.
  2. Account: Is this individual taxable, IRA or another account? Does it default to a cash account or margin?
  3. Recurring investing: Can it invest your desired dollar amount in your chosen ETF, not merely transfer cash?
  4. Costs: What are the ticker-specific trade, service, advisory, transfer-out and closure charges?
  5. Idle cash: What holds it, what yield does it earn, and what protection applies?
  6. Portability: Can whole shares transfer in kind? Fractional shares often need liquidation.
  7. Service: Can you reach a person to resolve verification, transfers or tax-document problems?
  8. Security: Are strong multifactor authentication, account alerts, trusted contacts and beneficiaries available?
BANK FAILURE

FDIC

Deposit insurance generally covers $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. Your same-category accounts at the same bank are added together. A bank sweep can qualify subject to its terms and your other deposits at participating banks. FDIC · Deposit insurance ↗

MISSING BROKERAGE ASSETS

SIPC

At a member broker, protection is generally up to $500,000, including $250,000 for cash, per customer in a separate capacity. It addresses missing assets in broker failure, not investment losses. Money market funds count as securities, not cash, for SIPC purposes. SIPC · What is protected ↗

The seven-point fund inspection
  1. Mandate: What index or strategy? Does it use leverage, options or swaps?
  2. Holdings: What countries, sectors, largest positions and overlaps? For bonds, check duration and credit quality.
  3. Structure: ETF, mutual fund, grantor trust, commodity pool or ETN? Exchange-listed does not mean they have identical protections or tax forms. An ETN is issuer debt and adds issuer credit risk. SEC · Exchange-traded notes ↗
  4. Costs: Expense ratio, any temporary waiver, spread, transaction or advisory fee.
  5. Execution: Typical spread, premium/discount history and liquidity of the underlying assets. Trading volume alone is not the whole story.
  6. Taxes: Distribution character, turnover, foreign tax credit, potential K-1 or special tax treatment.
  7. Fit: Write one sentence about its job. If you cannot explain why it improves the portfolio, skip it.

BUILD A PORTFOLIO

Simple can be complete.

Asset allocation—how much you hold in stocks, bonds and cash—usually matters more than choosing between two similar low-cost index funds.

0–3 years

Preserve the money

Insured savings, short Treasury bills and suitable CDs. Match maturity dates to spending dates. This includes your nine-month spending bucket.

3–7 years

Respect the deadline

Mostly safer assets if the spending date is firm; perhaps some stocks only if both timing and amount are flexible. A five-year horizon does not make stocks safe.

10+ years

Accept uncertainty

A diversified stock/bond portfolio can make sense. The stock share depends on your ability and willingness to sustain losses.

These are planning heuristics, not guarantees or regulatory rules. A retirement goal can remain long-term even if your residence changes.

A maintenance plan you can actually follow

  1. Automate after payday. Choose an amount that still leaves a cash buffer. Confirm that the automation buys the fund.
  2. Rebalance deliberately. Review once a year or when the stock share drifts about 5 percentage points from your target. These are optional policy rules, not precision science.
  3. Use new money first. Direct contributions toward underweight holdings before selling appreciated investments in a taxable account.
  4. Judge the whole portfolio. Include retirement accounts, taxable accounts, employer stock and other material assets.
  5. Change the plan when your life changes. A new spending need or reduced income is a reason to reassess. A scary headline alone is not a new investment horizon.
Lump sum or monthly?

Investing each paycheck is ordinary saving. If you already have a lump sum, investing sooner gives it more time in the market, while staging it over a short, precommitted period can reduce regret about timing. Staging does not guarantee a better price or eliminate losses once invested. Do not let a temporary schedule become indefinite waiting for the “right” market.

RETURNS & REAL COSTS

Control what compounds
against you.

You cannot choose next year’s return. You can choose how much you pay, how broadly you diversify, and how reliably you save.

The fund fee

An expense ratio is an annual cost taken from fund assets over time. You typically never see a separate bill. A 0.05% expense ratio is about $5 yearly per $10,000; 1% is about $100.

The trading friction

Commission-free does not mean cost-free. You can still pay the bid–ask spread, buy above net asset value, incur service fees or lose money to poor execution.

The service layer

An adviser or managed account might add a percentage or flat fee. It can be worthwhile for useful planning and behavioral support; compare the complete cost against the actual service.

Read yields without being misled

APY includes compounding for a deposit product; a money market fund’s 7-day yield annualizes recent income; a bond fund’s 30-day SEC yield uses a standardized calculation. A distribution yield describes payouts, which may include capital gains or return of capital. None is a promise of your future total return. Compare measures on the same date and after relevant taxes.

A California example: taxable bank interest versus Treasury interest

Suppose both yield 4%, your federal marginal rate is 24%, and your California marginal rate is 9.3%. Ignoring deductions, NIIT and other interactions: a fully taxable bank yield nets about 2.668% (4% × [1 − .24 − .093]); Treasury interest nets about 3.04% (4% × [1 − .24]). These are hypothetical rates, not current offers or your tax brackets. Treasury-fund distributions require the fund’s actual state-exempt percentage and state qualification, not a blanket assumption. How to buy your first direct Treasury bill
  1. In your broker’s fixed-income section, look for Treasury auctions/new issues. Alternatively, use TreasuryDirect if you meet its account requirements.
  2. Choose a bill with a maturity date before the expense, allowing time for the cash to reach your bank. Verify the security identifier (CUSIP), issue date and face amount.
  3. For a noncompetitive auction order, you accept the auction-determined rate; the final price is not known when you submit. Check minimums, increment size, order cutoff and fees.
  4. Keep the required funds available for settlement. A discount bill costing a hypothetical $990 and paying $1,000 at maturity earns $10 over that holding period—not a quoted 10% annual return.
  5. Confirm where maturity proceeds go and turn off automatic reinvestment when approaching your spending date. TreasuryDirect requires transfer to a broker to sell early and generally imposes a 45-day holding period on newly purchased securities.

Secondary-market purchases instead use quoted prices and yields. Review the total cost and maturity, not just the displayed yield. TreasuryDirect · Buying at auction ↗ · TreasuryDirect · Selling before maturity ↗

For long-term investing without an ETF

Low-cost index mutual funds

At Fidelity, examples include FSKAX (total US stocks, 0.015%), FXAIX (S&P 500, 0.015%) and FTIHX (total international stocks, 0.06%). These can be as sensible as ETFs, particularly in an IRA. Choose the matching exposure; FSKAX and FXAIX overlap heavily. Check other brokers’ purchase fees and whether a proprietary fund can transfer. Zero expense ratio does not automatically mean the best total fit. Fidelity · Index mutual funds ↗

Target-date index mutual funds

One fund combines stocks and bonds and adjusts the mix over time. Often the most convenient “buy one thing” retirement option. Read the stock percentage, glide path, underlying funds and fee. Target dates do not guarantee a balance or protect against losses. Taxable distributions can make these less appealing in a taxable account.

Individual bonds and TIPS

A Treasury ladder can match dated obligations. Longer Treasuries fluctuate more before maturity. Corporate bonds require credit analysis and diversification; a high coupon can signal a risky borrower. TIPS adjust principal with inflation, but market prices still change and taxable inflation adjustments can arrive before cash. Funds simplify diversification; direct bonds provide explicit maturity dates. FINRA · Bonds ↗ · TreasuryDirect · TIPS ↗

Individual stocks

You get precise control and no fund expense ratio, but take company-specific risk and spend time on research. Even a wonderful business can be a poor investment at an excessive price. You do not need individual stocks to participate in their success; broad funds already hold them.

PRIVATE EQUITY, UNPACKED

Access is not
the same as advantage.

Private equity (PE) owns businesses outside ordinary public stock markets. It can be a valid institutional allocation. It is usually a poor place to put your first investment dollar.

What PE actually does

Buyout funds acquire established companies, often using debt. Growth equity finances expanding businesses. Venture capital backs younger businesses with a high chance of failure and a small chance of very large outcomes. Investors usually commit capital to a manager rather than choose every company themselves. SEC · Private equity funds ↗

Why investors use it

Access to unlisted businesses, specialized managers and the possibility of improving companies over long holding periods. Those benefits must outweigh fees, financing risk, limited liquidity and the difficulty of picking a successful manager. There is no automatic “private-market premium” for every investor or fund.

What changes compared with a broad public ETF?

  • You surrender liquidity. Traditional drawdown funds commonly require many years, often around a decade with possible extensions. A secondary sale may require consent and a substantial discount.
  • You accept future funding obligations. A capital call requests part of your commitment. Keep unfunded commitments available; default provisions can be costly.
  • You pay multiple layers. Management fees may apply to committed or invested capital; carried interest gives the manager a share of profits. Fund expenses, financing costs and a feeder/platform fee can add more.
  • You see estimates, not continuous market prices. Infrequent appraisals can smooth reported returns. A smooth chart does not prove low economic risk.
  • You need to evaluate the manager. Portfolio leverage, vintage year, realized exits, key-person risk and allocation conflicts matter. Access to one fund does not replicate an endowment’s diversified PE program.

“2 and 20” is shorthand for a 2% management fee and 20% profit share, not a universal contract. Hypothetically, 2% of a $100,000 commitment is $2,000 for a year before other expenses and carry; the actual fee base, hurdle, catch-up and distribution waterfall can change the economics materially. CFA Institute · Economics of private equity ↗ · SEC · Private equity funds ↗

Do not compare a PE IRR to an ETF’s annual return without adjustments.

IRR depends heavily on when cash is called and returned and can be affected by subscription borrowing. Ask for net performance, realized distributions, the remaining estimated value, and a public-market comparison using equivalent cash-flow dates. Early costs can produce a “J-curve”; later gains are not guaranteed. CFA Institute · Private equity and the J-curve ↗ · CFA Institute · Economics of private equity ↗

Can an individual investor access it?

Many private funds limit participation to accredited investors and may impose additional eligibility and minimum-investment requirements. Common individual accreditation routes are net worth over $1 million excluding the primary residence, or income over $200,000 individually / $300,000 with a spouse or spousal equivalent in each of the prior two years with a reasonable expectation of the same this year. Certain professional credentials also qualify. A STEM degree or tax-resident status alone does not. Accreditation is a legal eligibility category, not a suitability endorsement. The due-diligence questions that should come before a commitment
  1. What do I legally own, who holds the assets, and who independently audits and values them?
  2. What is every fee, including the underlying funds, platform, financing, sales charges and profit-sharing waterfall?
  3. When can capital be called, what is the penalty for missing a call, and when can I realistically exit?
  4. What percentage of stated results is cash already returned versus unrealized estimates?
  5. What leverage exists at both the fund and company level?
  6. How does performance compare with public investments after matching cash-flow timing and net fees?
  7. Will I receive a 1099 or K-1, and could I owe tax without receiving cash or need extra state returns?
  8. Can my future residency prevent subscriptions, funding calls or redemptions?
  9. Why should this manager outperform for me, and what part of my portfolio can remain inaccessible for the entire term?

YOUR CALIFORNIA NOTES

Three differences worth knowing now

  1. No reduced capital-gains rate. California taxes capital gains as ordinary income; federal long-term treatment does not carry over as a lower state rate. California FTB · Capital gains ↗
  2. Treasury interest can help. Direct US Treasury interest is generally exempt from California income tax. For mutual funds and ETFs, use the issuer’s annual tax supplement and California eligibility rules, including the applicable 50% asset test. Do not assume a fund called “government” is fully state-exempt. California FTB · Income adjustments ↗ · California FTB · Resident adjustments ↗
  3. HSA treatment differs. Contributions and earnings require California adjustments. A federally tax-free account is not automatically state tax-free. California FTB · Resident adjustments ↗

A small tax example

You buy $1,000 of a fund and later sell all of those shares for $1,200, ignoring fees. Your gain is $200, not $1,200. If the gain qualifies for a 15% federal rate, that is $30 before state tax and other interactions. If you separately received and reinvested a $20 dividend, it can be taxable that year and creates $20 of additional basis in the new shares. Keep the purchases and tax lots straight to avoid taxing the same money twice.

Losses and wash sales

Capital losses first offset capital gains. A remaining net capital loss can generally offset up to $3,000 of other income per year ($1,500 married filing separately), with unused losses carried forward. IRS · Capital gains and losses ↗

A wash sale can disallow a loss when you buy the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the loss sale. Reinvestment, your other broker, an IRA and a spouse’s account can matter. An IRA replacement can cause permanent loss disallowance. Brokers may not catch cross-account transactions. Do not assume two differently named index funds are definitely safe substitutes. Fidelity · Wash-sale rules ↗ · IRS · Investment income and wash sales ↗

Tax efficiency without making this a second job

  • Favor low-turnover broad stock funds for taxable investing. Avoid unnecessary selling to chase recent winners.
  • Consider asset location only after allocation. Taxable bond income may fit tax-deferred accounts; broad equity funds may fit taxable accounts. Your account sizes, future tax rates and liquidity needs can override that rule of thumb.
  • Look at foreign-tax reporting. Some US-domiciled international funds can pass through a foreign tax credit in taxable accounts; check Form 1099-DIV and eligibility. It is not a reason by itself to add complexity.
  • Plan for the tax bill. Investment income often lacks payroll-style withholding. Large gains or distributions may require increased wage withholding or estimated payments.

YOUR FIRST PURCHASE

From bank balance
to invested balance.

A small first purchase is useful for learning the workflow. Keep the trade simple and verify each step.

  1. Open the correct account

    Use the broker’s official website. Prepare your SSN, legal name, date of birth, residential address, employment details and bank information. The broker may request passport and immigration documents. Identify citizenship and tax residency accurately. Choose a cash account for ordinary taxable investing; margin borrowing is unnecessary.

  2. Secure it before you fund it

    Use a unique password and strong multifactor authentication. Add transaction and login alerts. Name beneficiaries where appropriate and a trusted contact. Decline optional margin, options and securities-lending enrollment unless you understand and want them.

  3. Transfer a modest amount

    Link a bank account in your name. Confirm the amount available to trade and the amount available to withdraw; these can differ because of deposit holds. For an IRA contribution, select the correct tax year and check all contributions across institutions against your limit.

  4. Find the exact investment

    Search by ticker, then verify the full name, fund type, fee and exchange. Similar tickers can be completely different products. Read the current fund summary prospectus, especially the strategy and risks.

  5. Use a straightforward order

    For a liquid ETF, normal US exchange hours are generally 9:30 a.m.–4:00 p.m. Eastern on trading days. Avoid extended hours and chaotic opening/closing moments for a first trade. A market order prioritizes execution, not price. A limit order specifies your maximum buy price, but may not fill. Dollar-based fractional orders may have fewer order types.

  6. Preview, submit once, then verify

    Check the account, buy/sell direction, ticker, dollar amount or share count, order type, limit if any, time-in-force and charges. If using a limit, choose a sensible price near the current quote; do not assume a much lower limit will fill. Review pending orders before retrying.

  7. Confirm the position—not just the deposit

    Check filled versus partially filled versus open/canceled status, shares received, average price and remaining cash. Most US stocks and ETFs settle T+1, one business day after trading. Bank withdrawals and deposit holds can take additional time. Avoid selling securities bought with unsettled proceeds before those purchases are paid for. SEC · T+1 settlement ↗

  8. Make repetition easy

    Set a recurring investment only after the first purchase works. Choose dividend reinvestment or cash intentionally. Reinvestment can simplify compounding, but creates additional tax lots and can interfere with tax-loss harvesting. Save the confirmation and schedule your annual review.

The mistakes to recognize before they happen

  • Cash sitting uninvested: a funded Roth IRA with no fund purchase has not bought the market.
  • Buying every popular ticker: more tickers can add overlap, not diversification.
  • Borrowing to accelerate: margin adds interest and can force sales during a decline.

  • Chasing a story or recovery: “it has to come back” is not an investment thesis.

PLAIN-ENGLISH GLOSSARY

The terms, decoded.

Asset allocation
Your mix of stocks, bonds, cash and other assets.
Basis / tax lot
The tax cost of a holding; a lot is a specific purchase with its own cost and date.
Bid–ask spread
The gap between the best displayed buying and selling prices.
Capital gain
The amount realized on a sale above adjusted tax basis.
Capitalization
A company’s equity market value: share price × shares outstanding.
Compound return
Growth earned on both contributed money and previous growth.
Correlation
How returns tend to move together. It can change, especially in crises.
Distribution
A fund’s payment to shareholders; tax character can vary.
Domicile
For a fund, its legal home—not the countries where its investments operate.
Drawdown
A decline from a previous peak. Recovery can take years.
Duration
A bond’s interest-rate sensitivity. Roughly, duration 6 implies a 6% price decline for a 1-percentage-point yield rise, before other effects. FINRA · Duration and rate sensitivity ↗
Expense ratio
A fund’s recurring annual expenses as a percentage of assets.
Foreign tax credit
A potential credit for qualifying foreign taxes; eligibility and limits apply.
Index
A rules-based benchmark. Its construction determines its exposures.
Liquidity
How readily something can be sold for cash without a large price concession.
MAGI
Modified adjusted gross income; the exact modifications depend on the tax provision.
NAV
Net asset value: fund assets minus liabilities per share. Market price can differ.
Rebalancing
Restoring your chosen asset mix as markets and contributions change it.
Risk capacity
Your financial ability to bear losses; distinct from how comfortable you feel.
Settlement
The final exchange of cash and securities after a trade.
Total return
Price changes plus distributions, often measured assuming reinvestment.
Tracking difference
The gap between a fund’s return and its benchmark’s return.
Vesting
When an employer-provided benefit becomes yours to keep.
Volatility
The variability of prices or returns; one aspect of risk, not its entire meaning.

A BRIEF FINAL NOTE / IF YOU LEAVE THE US

Keep the investments.
Recheck the rules.

With about nine months left and Vietnam as home, revisit these points well before departure. Moving is not automatically a reason to liquidate, nor does leaving on a flight immediately end tax residency.

  1. Confirm broker service for a Vietnam address. Ask in writing whether you can retain each account, buy and sell your holdings, reinvest distributions, receive cash and transfer out. Do not keep a false US address. Fidelity, for example, limits services abroad and generally restricts new mutual-fund purchases. Country and account rules differ. Fidelity · Accounts and overseas restrictions ↗
  2. Review US, California and Vietnamese tax residency together. A departure year can be dual-status federally; California has its own residency analysis. Update W-9/W-8BEN only when warranted by your actual status. Vietnam is not listed in the IRS’s current in-force income-tax treaty directory; do not assume treaty-reduced US dividend withholding. For nonresidents, US dividends generally face 30% withholding absent an applicable exception or treaty. Get advice on Vietnamese reporting, foreign tax credits and treatment of US retirement accounts. IRS · Tax residency ↗ · IRS · Tax guide for aliens ↗ · IRS · In-force income-tax treaties ↗
  3. Review estate-tax exposure before a large balance. Income-tax residency and estate-tax domicile are different tests. A noncitizen not domiciled in the US may face a US estate-return threshold generally starting above $60,000 of US-situated assets, subject to detailed rules and treaties. This can matter even before departure. US stocks and US-domiciled funds warrant review; a foreign broker does not by itself remove US situs. IRS · Estate tax for noncitizens ↗ · IRS · US-situated estate assets ↗
  4. Preserve access and records. Keep secure authentication usable abroad, accurate contact details, beneficiaries, basis and contribution history. Arrange cash for the move. Do not reflexively cash out retirement accounts or swap into foreign-domiciled funds while still a US tax resident.

The near-term action: build the US investing foundation now, and schedule a focused broker and cross-border tax review before your move.
