BEYOND ETFS

Other good options.
And where they fit.

ETFs are convenient packaging, not the only sensible way to invest. Some of your most useful first choices are not ETFs at all.

How to buy your first direct Treasury bill
  1. In your broker’s fixed-income section, look for Treasury auctions/new issues. Alternatively, use TreasuryDirect if you meet its account requirements.
  2. Choose a bill with a maturity date before the expense, allowing time for the cash to reach your bank. Verify the security identifier (CUSIP), issue date and face amount.
  3. For a noncompetitive auction order, you accept the auction-determined rate; the final price is not known when you submit. Check minimums, increment size, order cutoff and fees.
  4. Keep the required funds available for settlement. A discount bill costing a hypothetical $990 and paying $1,000 at maturity earns $10 over that holding period—not a quoted 10% annual return.
  5. Confirm where maturity proceeds go and turn off automatic reinvestment when approaching your spending date. TreasuryDirect requires transfer to a broker to sell early and generally imposes a 45-day holding period on newly purchased securities.

Secondary-market purchases instead use quoted prices and yields. Review the total cost and maturity, not just the displayed yield. [[buybill,sellbill]]

For long-term investing without an ETF

Low-cost index mutual funds

At Fidelity, examples include FSKAX (total US stocks, 0.015%), FXAIX (S&P 500, 0.015%) and FTIHX (total international stocks, 0.06%). These can be as sensible as ETFs, particularly in an IRA. Choose the matching exposure; FSKAX and FXAIX overlap heavily. Check other brokers’ purchase fees and whether a proprietary fund can transfer. Zero expense ratio does not automatically mean the best total fit. [[findex]]

Target-date index mutual funds

One fund combines stocks and bonds and adjusts the mix over time. Often the most convenient “buy one thing” retirement option. Read the stock percentage, glide path, underlying funds and fee. Target dates do not guarantee a balance or protect against losses. Taxable distributions can make these less appealing in a taxable account.

Individual bonds and TIPS

A Treasury ladder can match dated obligations. Longer Treasuries fluctuate more before maturity. Corporate bonds require credit analysis and diversification; a high coupon can signal a risky borrower. TIPS adjust principal with inflation, but market prices still change and taxable inflation adjustments can arrive before cash. Funds simplify diversification; direct bonds provide explicit maturity dates. [[bonds,tips]]

Individual stocks

You get precise control and no fund expense ratio, but take company-specific risk and spend time on research. Even a wonderful business can be a poor investment at an excessive price. You do not need individual stocks to participate in their success; broad funds already hold them.

A practical rule: if you want a learning allocation for individual stocks or speculative assets, cap it at an amount you can lose entirely without harming your goals. A self-imposed 0–5% of long-term investments is one possible guardrail, not a requirement or a recommendation to use the full allowance.

PRIVATE EQUITY, UNPACKED

Access is not
the same as advantage.

Private equity (PE) owns businesses outside ordinary public stock markets. It can be a valid institutional allocation. It is usually a poor place to put your first investment dollar.

What PE actually does

Buyout funds acquire established companies, often using debt. Growth equity finances expanding businesses. Venture capital backs younger businesses with a high chance of failure and a small chance of very large outcomes. Investors usually commit capital to a manager rather than choose every company themselves. [[pe]]

Why investors use it

Access to unlisted businesses, specialized managers and the possibility of improving companies over long holding periods. Those benefits must outweigh fees, financing risk, limited liquidity and the difficulty of picking a successful manager. There is no automatic “private-market premium” for every investor or fund.

What changes compared with a broad public ETF?

  • You surrender liquidity. Traditional drawdown funds commonly require many years, often around a decade with possible extensions. A secondary sale may require consent and a substantial discount.
  • You accept future funding obligations. A capital call requests part of your commitment. Keep unfunded commitments available; default provisions can be costly.
  • You pay multiple layers. Management fees may apply to committed or invested capital; carried interest gives the manager a share of profits. Fund expenses, financing costs and a feeder/platform fee can add more.
  • You see estimates, not continuous market prices. Infrequent appraisals can smooth reported returns. A smooth chart does not prove low economic risk.
  • You need to evaluate the manager. Portfolio leverage, vintage year, realized exits, key-person risk and allocation conflicts matter. Access to one fund does not replicate an endowment’s diversified PE program.

“2 and 20” is shorthand for a 2% management fee and 20% profit share, not a universal contract. Hypothetically, 2% of a $100,000 commitment is $2,000 for a year before other expenses and carry; the actual fee base, hurdle, catch-up and distribution waterfall can change the economics materially. [[peeconomics,pe]]

Do not compare a PE IRR to an ETF’s annual return without adjustments.

IRR depends heavily on when cash is called and returned and can be affected by subscription borrowing. Ask for net performance, realized distributions, the remaining estimated value, and a public-market comparison using equivalent cash-flow dates. Early costs can produce a “J-curve”; later gains are not guaranteed. [[pej,peeconomics]]

Can an individual investor access it?

Many private funds limit participation to accredited investors and may impose additional eligibility and minimum-investment requirements. Common individual accreditation routes are net worth over $1 million excluding the primary residence, or income over $200,000 individually / $300,000 with a spouse or spousal equivalent in each of the prior two years with a reasonable expectation of the same this year. Certain professional credentials also qualify. A STEM degree or tax-resident status alone does not. Accreditation is a legal eligibility category, not a suitability endorsement. [[accredited]]

The due-diligence questions that should come before a commitment
  1. What do I legally own, who holds the assets, and who independently audits and values them?
  2. What is every fee, including the underlying funds, platform, financing, sales charges and profit-sharing waterfall?
  3. When can capital be called, what is the penalty for missing a call, and when can I realistically exit?
  4. What percentage of stated results is cash already returned versus unrealized estimates?
  5. What leverage exists at both the fund and company level?
  6. How does performance compare with public investments after matching cash-flow timing and net fees?
  7. Will I receive a 1099 or K-1, and could I owe tax without receiving cash or need extra state returns?
  8. Can my future residency prevent subscriptions, funding calls or redemptions?
  9. Why should this manager outperform for me, and what part of my portfolio can remain inaccessible for the entire term?
FOR YOUR FIRST DOLLAR

A 0% private-equity allocation is a complete decision.

With a short remaining US stay and an unbuilt public-market foundation, preserve flexibility. Revisit PE only after your reserves, tax planning and diversified long-term portfolio are established—and only with genuinely expendable, patient capital.

YOUR CALIFORNIA NOTES

Three differences worth knowing now

  1. No reduced capital-gains rate. California taxes capital gains as ordinary income; federal long-term treatment does not carry over as a lower state rate. [[cagains]]
  2. Treasury interest can help. Direct US Treasury interest is generally exempt from California income tax. For mutual funds and ETFs, use the issuer’s annual tax supplement and California eligibility rules, including the applicable 50% asset test. Do not assume a fund called “government” is fully state-exempt. [[ca1001,caadjust]]
  3. HSA treatment differs. Contributions and earnings require California adjustments. A federally tax-free account is not automatically state tax-free. [[caadjust]]

A small tax example

You buy $1,000 of a fund and later sell all of those shares for $1,200, ignoring fees. Your gain is $200, not $1,200. If the gain qualifies for a 15% federal rate, that is $30 before state tax and other interactions. If you separately received and reinvested a $20 dividend, it can be taxable that year and creates $20 of additional basis in the new shares. Keep the purchases and tax lots straight to avoid taxing the same money twice.

Losses and wash sales

Capital losses first offset capital gains. A remaining net capital loss can generally offset up to $3,000 of other income per year ($1,500 married filing separately), with unused losses carried forward. [[gains]]

A wash sale can disallow a loss when you buy the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the loss sale. Reinvestment, your other broker, an IRA and a spouse’s account can matter. An IRA replacement can cause permanent loss disallowance. Brokers may not catch cross-account transactions. Do not assume two differently named index funds are definitely safe substitutes. [[wash,550]]

Tax efficiency without making this a second job

  • Favor low-turnover broad stock funds for taxable investing. Avoid unnecessary selling to chase recent winners.
  • Consider asset location only after allocation. Taxable bond income may fit tax-deferred accounts; broad equity funds may fit taxable accounts. Your account sizes, future tax rates and liquidity needs can override that rule of thumb.
  • Look at foreign-tax reporting. Some US-domiciled international funds can pass through a foreign tax credit in taxable accounts; check Form 1099-DIV and eligibility. It is not a reason by itself to add complexity.
  • Plan for the tax bill. Investment income often lacks payroll-style withholding. Large gains or distributions may require increased wage withholding or estimated payments.

Do my existing Vietnamese accounts matter while I am a US tax resident?

Yes. US tax residents generally report worldwide income, including interest or investment income kept abroad. US brokerage-only investing does not remove obligations for existing foreign accounts. [[519]]

FBAR reporting is generally triggered when aggregate foreign financial accounts exceed $10,000 at any time in the year. Form 8938 is separate; for an unmarried filer living in the US, relevant foreign assets generally trigger it above $50,000 at year-end or $75,000 at any time, subject to filing requirements. Other filing statuses and residence situations have different thresholds. [[foreign]]

Foreign-domiciled mutual funds and ETFs can be PFICs, producing complex Form 8621 obligations and unfavorable tax results. A US-domiciled international fund such as VXUS is a different structure. Before buying or selling existing overseas funds, ask a cross-border tax professional; a sale can itself trigger consequences. [[pfic]]
